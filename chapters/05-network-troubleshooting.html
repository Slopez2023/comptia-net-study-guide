Network Troubleshooting

Everything you've learned, used as a symptom → cause → fix chain. Memorize the 7 steps word for word, then learn to read a symptom and pick the tool.

  • 5 lessons

Recite CompTIA's 7 troubleshooting steps in order, and answer "what should the tech do NEXT?" questions.

The job: Troubleshooting turns a vague report such as "the network is down" into evidence, a safe fix, and a record another technician can use. The order matters because a quick guess can damage a working service or hide the real cause.

Identify the problem

Start by gathering facts before touching a setting. Ask who is affected, what they were trying to do, when the failure began, and whether anything changed. Identify observable symptoms, such as a missing link light, an APIPA address, or a web page that fails while other sites work. If practical, duplicate the problem so you know it is real and repeatable. When several incidents arrive at once, approach multiple problems individually until evidence shows they share one cause.

The first step is not "restart it." A restart may make a symptom disappear before you record it. Record scope, time, error messages, recent changes, and the user impact first.

Establish a theory of probable cause

Make a short, testable explanation from the evidence. Question the obvious, because a disconnected cable and a disabled switch port can produce the same no-link complaint. You may work the OSI model top-to-bottom, beginning with the application, or bottom-to-top, beginning with power and cabling. Divide and conquer starts in the middle, such as checking IP configuration before deciding whether the fault is physical or application-related.

Test the theory to determine the cause

Run the least disruptive test that can prove or reject the theory. If a user cannot reach an internal server, a ping to the default gateway checks a different point than a ping to the server. When the test confirms the theory, move to the next steps. When it does not, form a new theory from the result or escalate if another team owns the system.

A test needs a predicted result. "I think DNS is broken" becomes "an IP-address request works, but nslookup for the same host fails." That result supports the DNS theory.

Establish a plan of action and identify potential effects

Choose a repair and think through what it can change. Moving a switch port to another VLAN may restore one device but remove its access to a printer. A maintenance window, rollback plan, backup, or approval may be needed. This step is where change management protects users from an impulsive fix.

Implement the solution or escalate as necessary

Make the approved change and keep track of exactly what you changed. Escalate when the repair requires authority, access, expertise, or a provider action that you do not have. Escalating is not failing the process. It gives the next team useful evidence instead of a vague ticket.

Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures

Do more than check whether the original complaint disappeared. Test the user task, related services, and other affected users. Then prevent a repeat when possible. That might mean replacing a damaged patch cable, adding monitoring to a nearly full DHCP scope, or labeling a port correctly.

Verification comes before documentation. A configuration change that appears to work for one ping has not yet proved full system functionality.

Document findings, actions, outcomes, and lessons learned

Write the symptom, evidence, root cause, commands or changes made, verification results, and preventive work. Clear notes shorten the next outage because another technician can compare new evidence with a known incident.

Analogy

Think of a mechanic with a car that will not start. They ask about the symptom, form a theory, test the battery, plan the repair, make it, road-test the car, and record the work. A network has more shared parts, so changing one setting can affect many users. That is where the analogy stops matching.

  1. Maya learns that one department cannot print, while other departments can. The outage began after a desk move.
  2. She checks the printer's address and finds it now has 169.254.18.7. Her theory is that this VLAN cannot reach DHCP.

  4. She corrects the port assignment, renews the lease, prints a test page, checks the adjacent workstation, and records the incorrect port label for follow-up.

Read interface counters and port status, and diagnose cable, PoE and transceiver problems.

The job: Physical faults often look like slow applications or broken addressing. Check the medium, its condition, interface evidence, power, and optics before replacing equipment.

Incorrect cable and signal degradation

Match the medium to the link. Single-mode and multimode fiber need compatible optics and fiber types. A category rating, run length, and target speed must also agree. Category 5e commonly supports 1 GbE over a 100-meter run, while faster copper needs the right category and distance. Use shielded twisted pair where electromagnetic interference is likely. Unshielded twisted pair is common in ordinary office runs.

Crosstalk is unwanted signal between pairs, often from poor termination. External interference can come from motors or power gear. Attenuation is signal loss over distance or through damage, bends, and dirty fiber ends.

Start with the simple physical path: correct medium and rating, supported length, secure termination, and clean, matched fiber ends.

Cable issues and interface counters

An improper termination can leave a pair open, shorted, or incorrectly ordered. A cable tester finds those faults. TX/RX transposed means each end uses the wrong pair or fiber strand. Auto-MDIX resolves many copper crossover cases, but bad fiber polarity still prevents communication.

Interface counters show what the port sees. CRC errors mean corrupted frames arrived. Runts are frames smaller than the Ethernet minimum and can indicate collisions or duplex trouble. Giants exceed the normal frame size and may point to an MTU mismatch. Drops mean the device discarded traffic, often because a queue or buffer filled. Look for counters that rise during the complaint, not old totals.

Rising CRC errors on one access port point to its cable, connector, or local interference. Rising drops across busy ports point more strongly to congestion or capacity.

Port status and PoE

Error disabled means the switch shut a port after a condition such as a port-security violation or BPDU Guard event. Fix the trigger first. Administratively down means configuration disabled the port. Suspended often means a link aggregation member does not match its partner.

For PoE, ask both whether the port offers the needed standard and whether the switch has enough total power. A camera may require 802.3at or 802.3bt even though the port offers 802.3af. A new device can fail when the total power budget is exhausted.

Transceivers

Both fiber ends need compatible speed, wavelength, connector, and fiber type. Low received optical power can mean a dirty connector, bend, excessive loss, or a run longer than the optic supports.

Link up does not prove the physical layer is healthy. A marginal cable can negotiate a link and still cause CRC errors, retransmissions, and poor throughput.

Analogy

A cable path resembles a water hose. Its fittings must match, it cannot be pinched, and the pump must have enough capacity. Network signals are electrical or optical, so counters and light levels provide evidence that a hose cannot.

Diagnose STP, VLAN, ACL, routing and addressing problems from symptoms.

The job: Service failures often leave a useful boundary. Find the last point that works, then inspect the service responsible for the next hop rather than changing every setting at once.

STP issues

Redundant switch links improve availability but can create a Layer 2 loop. A loop multiplies broadcasts and can make MAC addresses appear on alternating ports. Spanning Tree Protocol blocks selected redundant paths so only one forwarding path remains. The root bridge is the reference switch for that tree. If an old access switch becomes root, traffic may take an inefficient route. Set the intended core switch to a lower bridge priority.

Port roles describe each port's job. A root port is a non-root switch's best path toward the root. A designated port forwards for a segment. An alternate port provides a blocked backup path. Port states describe behavior, such as forwarding or discarding. A port that should forward but remains in a blocking or discarding state may reflect an STP topology or configuration issue.

MAC flapping, widespread slow traffic, and broadcast storms make a loop likely. Check STP state before disabling it, because disabling STP can turn one mistake into an outage.

Switching issues

An access port in the wrong VLAN places a device in the wrong broadcast domain. The device may receive an address from the wrong subnet or fail to reach expected local resources. Check the port assignment and the VLAN allowed on any trunk. An ACL can block only selected traffic. If ping by IP works but a particular application port fails, inspect the ACL direction, order, addresses, ports, and implicit deny.

Wrong VLAN assignment changes where a host belongs. An ACL filters traffic that tries to cross a policy point. The symptoms can overlap, but the evidence differs.

Route selection

A router uses its routing table to find a matching network prefix and chooses the most specific match first. If no specific route matches, it may use a default route. Missing or incorrect default routes often allow local communication but block remote networks or the internet. Check the table before adding routes. An existing more-specific route may already explain the path.

Addressing

Address pool exhaustion prevents DHCP from assigning another lease. New clients may receive an APIPA address or no usable address. A wrong default gateway lets a host reach its own subnet but not remote networks. A duplicate IP can cause intermittent failures as ARP entries switch between two MAC addresses. A wrong subnet mask makes a host believe a remote address is local, or a local address is remote, so it sends traffic to the wrong place.

Use the symptom boundary. Local only works but remote fails suggests the gateway or route. An APIPA address suggests DHCP. Intermittent conflict messages suggest duplicate addressing.

Analogy

Think of a building directory. VLANs put people on the correct floor, ACLs decide which doors they may use, and routing tells a courier which building to visit next. Packets use tables and headers rather than signs, so the analogy does not describe the actual forwarding process.

Tell latency, jitter, packet loss and bandwidth problems apart, and fix common Wi-Fi issues.

The job: Performance troubleshooting asks what resource or condition limits the user experience. A slow transfer, a late voice packet, and an unreliable Wi-Fi call can look alike until you measure delay, variation, loss, capacity, and radio conditions.

Congestion, contention, and bottlenecking

Congestion occurs when offered traffic exceeds a link or device's ability to forward it. Queues grow and devices may drop packets. Contention is competition for a shared medium, especially Wi-Fi, where clients take turns transmitting. A bottleneck is the slowest resource along a path. A 10 GbE server link cannot deliver 10 GbE to a user whose access port is 1 GbE.

Bandwidth is the rated capacity of a link. Throughput is the useful rate actually delivered. Protocol overhead, loss, retries, device limits, and contention reduce throughput. Measure at several points before assuming an internet circuit is the only limit.

Capacity symptoms often appear at predictable busy times. Compare current throughput, interface utilization, and drops with a baseline instead of treating a speed-test result as the whole answer.

Latency, packet loss, and jitter

Latency is how long a packet takes to travel. Distance, queueing, processing, and slow paths add delay. Packet loss means some packets never arrive. TCP retransmits missing packets, which can make web and file work feel slow. Real-time voice and video cannot wait for every retransmission, so loss creates gaps.

Jitter is variation in packet delay. A call may have acceptable average latency but still sound choppy when packets arrive at uneven intervals. QoS can prioritize delay-sensitive traffic, but it cannot create more physical capacity. Remove congestion or increase capacity when the link is truly full.

Latency is a delay value. Jitter is inconsistency in delay. Packet loss is absence. A question that mentions choppy voice and uneven arrival times is usually testing jitter.

Wireless interference and coverage

Wi-Fi clients share radio channels. Nearby access points on overlapping channels create contention and interference. On 2.4 GHz, channels 1, 6, and 11 avoid overlap in common channel plans. A Wi-Fi analyzer and site survey show channel use, signal levels, and noisy areas. Signal degradation or loss can come from walls, distance, interference, or poor antenna placement. Strong signal alone does not rule out interference.

Insufficient coverage leaves areas where the signal is too weak for a stable connection. Client disassociation can occur after weak signal, interference, authentication trouble, or a deauthentication attack. Roaming misconfiguration causes calls to drop when a device moves between APs. Keep the intended SSID and security consistent, then tune placement and transmit power so clients can move predictably.

For wireless complaints, first distinguish weak coverage from a crowded channel. Adding an AP can improve coverage but worsen contention if its channel plan is poor.

Analogy

A busy road helps separate these terms. Bandwidth is lane capacity, congestion is too many cars, latency is travel time, jitter is an unpredictable trip length, and loss is a car that never arrives. Packets do not drive, and Wi-Fi clients must share radio airtime, so the analogy stops there.

Pick the right command, software tool, hardware tool or device show command for a problem.

The job: A troubleshooting tool is useful when its output answers a specific question. Pick the tool that tests the suspected boundary, then use its result to choose the next test.

Software tools

A protocol analyzer such as Wireshark decodes captured packets. Use it when you need to see whether DNS replies arrive, whether a TCP connection resets, or what protocol a host actually sends. Capture only the traffic you are authorized to inspect. Nmap discovers hosts and checks reachable ports and services. It is a scan, so use it only with permission.

LLDP and CDP identify directly connected neighbors. LLDP is open standard; CDP is Cisco proprietary. They help trace an unknown uplink or confirm the device on the other end of a port. A speed tester measures throughput. It helps establish whether the useful data rate matches expectations, but it does not identify every cause of a slow application.

Use a packet analyzer for packet contents, Nmap for authorized host and port discovery, LLDP or CDP for immediate neighbors, and a speed tester for measured throughput.

Command-line tools

ping tests IP reachability with ICMP echo. It does not prove that every application works. traceroute on many Unix-like systems, or tracert on Windows, shows the routed hops toward a destination and can suggest where a path changes or stops responding. Routers may filter those probes, so a missing reply is evidence to investigate, not automatic proof of a failed router.

nslookup and dig test DNS resolution and records. tcpdump captures packets at the command line. ip, ifconfig, and ipconfig display interface addressing. arp shows IP-to-MAC cache entries. netstat displays connections, listeners, or routing information depending on its options and platform.

Test in layers. Check local addressing and gateway, ping an IP address, trace the route if needed, then test DNS. This sequence narrows the failure without assuming DNS is always the culprit.

Hardware tools

A toner and probe identifies a particular copper cable in a bundle. A cable tester checks opens, shorts, split pairs, and pinout. A tap copies traffic to a monitoring device without making that device part of the normal endpoint conversation. A Wi-Fi analyzer displays channels, signal strength, and nearby networks. A visual fault locator sends visible light through fiber to reveal a break or sharp bend.

Choose the hardware tool by the question. Use a toner to find a cable, a tester to assess its wiring, a Wi-Fi analyzer to inspect radio use, and a visual fault locator to find a fiber defect.

Device commands

show mac-address-table maps learned MAC addresses to ports and VLANs. show route or show ip route shows routes. show interface reports status, speed, duplex, and counters. show config or show running-config displays active configuration. show arp displays the device ARP cache. show vlan lists VLAN membership. show power or show power inline reports PoE availability and draw. Exact command syntax varies by vendor.

Analogy

Tools resemble items in a mechanic's box. A voltmeter, a camera, and a wrench can all help with a car, but each answers a different question. Network tools produce evidence rather than fixing the network by themselves.
