Network Operations

This chapter covers keeping a network running: documenting it, monitoring it, recovering it, and the services every device depends on (DHCP, DNS, time). DNS records and DHCP are tested heavily.

Know which document answers which question, and how life-cycle, change and configuration management work.

The job: Operations work goes faster and causes less risk when people can find the network's current facts, make controlled changes, and retire old equipment safely.

Documentation answers a specific question

A network diagram is useful only when it matches the question in front of you. A physical diagram shows rooms, racks, switches, and cables. A logical diagram shows subnets, VLANs, routing, and traffic paths. If a server cannot reach another VLAN, start with the logical view. If a desk jack is dead, use the physical view or cable map.

A rack diagram records what occupies each rack unit. A cable map connects a patch-panel port, switch port, and wall jack. Layer 1, 2, and 3 diagrams narrow the view to cabling, switching, or IP routing. Keeping those views separate prevents a crowded drawing from hiding the answer.

Other records describe what the diagrams cannot. An asset inventory tracks hardware, installed software, licenses, ownership, warranties, and replacement dates. IP address management, or IPAM, records address ranges, assigned addresses, reservations, and available space. A service-level agreement, or SLA, states the provider's promised availability, response, or repair target. A wireless survey and heat map show where signal quality and capacity are likely to be good or poor.

Manage the equipment life cycle

Equipment and software have a planned life, even when they still power on. End of life, or EOL, means the vendor has stopped selling a product. End of support, or EOS, is the more urgent operations point because the vendor no longer supplies support or security fixes. Track both dates so a replacement is a scheduled project rather than an outage-day surprise.

Software management includes planned updates to operating systems, firmware, and patches. Test an update first, schedule it, and record the installed version. When decommissioning a device, back up any needed configuration, securely erase or destroy data, remove its access, and update the asset inventory and diagrams.

Analogy

Think of a building maintenance office. Floor plans locate a room, an equipment list records what is installed, and a work order records a repair. Network documents work the same way. The comparison stops at the network's changing traffic and security settings, which need their own logical records.

Make changes repeatable

Change management gives a normal change a traceable path. Someone opens and tracks a request or service request, explains the reason and impact, gets the needed approval, schedules a window, and prepares a rollback plan. After the work, the team verifies the result and records what actually changed. A change advisory board may review changes with broad risk, but not every organization uses the same approval group.

Configuration management records the intended and actual settings of a device. The production configuration is the one currently in use. A backup configuration is a saved copy that can restore a known working state. A baseline or golden configuration is the approved standard used to spot drift before it becomes an outage or security gap.

Do not treat a device backup as the golden configuration. A backup may faithfully preserve an old mistake. The golden configuration is the reviewed target. Compare production against it, then make approved corrections through change management.

A story: tracing a failed desk connection

  1. Nora reports that the jack at desk 204 has no link light after an office move.
  2. The technician checks the cable map to find the wall jack, patch-panel position, and switch port, then uses the physical diagram to locate the correct rack.
  3. After replacing the damaged patch cable, the technician checks the logical diagram and IPAM record to confirm the port belongs to the expected VLAN and subnet.
  4. The team records the repair in the service request and updates the cable map, so the next technician starts with current information.

  • Logical diagram: VLANs, subnets, and routed relationships.
  • EOL: no longer sold.
  • EOS: no more vendor support or fixes.

Pick the right monitoring tool: SNMP, flow data, packet capture, syslog/SIEM, or port mirroring.

The job: Monitoring turns device behavior into evidence, so an administrator can spot a problem, measure its effect, and choose the right next check.

Ask devices for status

SNMP lets a monitoring system collect values from a managed device. The device runs an agent and exposes named values in a management information base, or MIB. A network management system can poll the agent for interface errors, bandwidth use, temperature, or CPU load. An SNMP trap reverses the direction. The device sends an unsolicited alert when an event occurs, such as a link going down.

SNMPv2c uses a community string, which is not safe protection because it travels in clear text. SNMPv3 supports authentication and encryption. Use version 3 when possible, and limit which management hosts may reach the service.

Choose the right evidence

Flow data, such as NetFlow or IPFIX, summarizes conversations. It can show source and destination addresses, ports, protocol, timing, and byte counts without saving every packet. It answers questions such as "Which hosts used the most bandwidth?" A packet capture records the packets themselves and helps diagnose a handshake, retransmission, DNS response, or application conversation. Captures can contain private data, so collect and retain them carefully.

Port mirroring, also called SPAN, copies traffic from a switch port or VLAN to an analyzer port. It creates the observation point for a capture tool or intrusion-detection system. It does not itself interpret packets. A syslog collector centralizes event messages. A SIEM ingests logs from many sources, normalizes or correlates them, and raises security-focused alerts. An API integration moves data between monitoring, ticketing, and dashboard systems without manual copying.

First establish a baseline for ordinary traffic, response time, errors, and availability. Anomaly alerting compares current measurements with that normal range. A high number means little without a baseline, because a busy payroll window may be normal on one network and suspicious on another.

Monitor the job, not a single number

Network discovery finds devices and services. An ad hoc scan helps during an investigation; a scheduled scan keeps inventory current. Traffic analysis studies who communicates and how much. Performance monitoring follows measures such as latency, loss, utilization, and interface errors. Availability monitoring checks whether a service, device, or path responds. Configuration monitoring detects changes to settings, versions, or policies.

Use more than one signal before declaring the cause. A failed availability check might mean a service is down, a firewall blocks the probe, or the monitoring path itself is broken. Pair the result with interface counters, recent configuration changes, logs, and a test from a second location. Alert thresholds should create tickets that people can act on, rather than a flood of expected events.

Analogy

A monitoring room resembles a hospital's records desk. A regular vital-sign check is polling, an alarm is a trap, and a detailed lab sample is a packet capture. The analogy stops there because a network device can report counters and logs without a person measuring it.

A story: finding the top talker

  1. At 10:00, remote users report sluggish access to the file server.
  2. The monitoring dashboard compares WAN utilization with its baseline and shows a sharp increase.
  3. Flow data identifies one workstation sending a large volume to an unfamiliar cloud address. The team does not start with a full packet capture because the flow summary already identifies the conversation.
  4. They open a ticket, inspect the workstation, and use the syslog and SIEM records to determine whether the transfer violated policy.
Keep these separate
  • Trap: an event sent by the device without a poll.
  • Poll: a monitoring system asks for a value.
  • Flow data: conversation metadata and volume.
  • Packet capture: packet-level detail, including payload when visible.

Use RPO, RTO, MTTR and MTBF correctly, and match cold, warm and hot sites to budget and recovery speed.

The job: Disaster recovery sets a realistic promise for how much data the organization can lose and how long a service may remain unavailable.

Set the data and downtime targets

Recovery point objective, or RPO, is the maximum acceptable gap in data. An RPO of one hour means the organization can accept losing up to one hour of changes, so backups or replication must meet that target. Recovery time objective, or RTO, is the maximum acceptable time to restore the service after a disruption. It includes the work needed to detect the incident, recover systems, test them, and return service to users.

RPO looks backward from the disruption to the last usable data point. RTO looks forward from the disruption to restored service. A short RPO does not automatically create a short RTO. Frequent backups may limit data loss while a complex rebuild still takes many hours.

Mean time to repair, or MTTR, measures how long repair usually takes. Lower is better. Mean time between failures, or MTBF, estimates the interval between failures of a repairable component. Higher is better. These measures help with planning and reliability discussions, but they do not replace a stated RTO or RPO.

Choose how ready the recovery site must be

A cold site has a location, power, and connectivity but little or no installed equipment. It costs least and takes the longest to use. A warm site has equipment in place and may have some data or configurations available, but it still needs work before it can take production traffic. A hot site is already equipped and kept close to current, so it can recover service fastest at the highest ongoing cost.

Match the site to the business target. A public safety dispatch application may justify a hot site. An internal archival system may tolerate the cost and delay of a cold site. The best answer depends on the required RPO and RTO, not on which site sounds most impressive.

Design for continued service

High availability reduces the effect of a component failure. In an active-active design, more than one node handles production work at the same time. In active-passive, one node handles work while the standby waits to take over. Active-passive can be simpler, but the standby and failover process still need testing.

A recovery plan also lists dependencies and the order in which to restore them. A web application may require DNS, identity services, storage, routing, certificates, and a database before its page is useful. Backups must be readable, access to the recovery site must be available, and contact lists must be current. Those practical checks turn a stated target into a plan the team can carry out.

Analogy

A recovery plan resembles a fire plan for an office. It states which records matter, how long occupants can be out, and where they can work instead. The analogy stops there because network services must also restore data, configurations, and dependent systems in the right order.

A story: recovering payroll

  1. Payroll can lose at most 15 minutes of entries and must be available again within four hours. Those are its RPO and RTO.
  2. The team replicates its database often enough to meet the 15-minute RPO and maintains a warm recovery site with documented network settings.
  3. During an outage, they restore the application and data, test a login and report, then direct users to the recovered service.
  4. A validation test later proves the measured recovery time meets the four-hour target. A discussion-only tabletop exercise would not prove the technical steps.

Configure DHCP (scope, reservations, relay), explain SLAAC, read DNS records, and know DNSSEC, DoH/DoT and the time protocols.

The job: A client needs usable network settings, name resolution, and accurate time before it can reliably reach services and produce useful security records.

Give an IPv4 client its settings

DHCP can assign an IPv4 address along with the subnet mask, default gateway, DNS servers, time servers, and other options. A scope defines the address pool and related settings for one subnet. An exclusion keeps particular addresses inside that range from being handed out, such as addresses set aside for infrastructure. A lease time limits how long a client may use an address before renewing it.

A reservation connects a known client identifier, commonly its MAC address, with a chosen address. Use it when a printer needs a predictable address while still receiving settings from DHCP. Do not reserve the address by simply placing it in the dynamic pool without a reservation or exclusion.

A new client commonly follows DORA: Discover, Offer, Request, and Acknowledge. It begins with a broadcast because the client does not know the server. Routers do not normally forward broadcasts. For a centrally located server to serve another VLAN, the router or Layer 3 switch uses a DHCP relay, often called an IP helper, to forward the request as a unicast message.

Let IPv6 clients form addresses

SLAAC gives an IPv6 host a different path. A router advertisement announces a prefix and default-router information. The host combines that prefix with an interface identifier, checks for a duplicate address, and can use the result. The host also has a link-local IPv6 address for its local link. SLAAC can operate with DHCPv6 when a network needs centrally supplied settings or addresses.

DHCP gives a client settings or an address from a server-controlled pool. SLAAC forms an IPv6 address from router information. Neither one translates a website name. That is DNS.

Resolve names and know who answered

DNS maps names to data such as IP addresses. A recursive resolver performs lookups on a client's behalf and may return an answer from its cache. An authoritative server holds the records for its zone and gives the source answer. A non-authoritative answer comes from a cache. The primary zone copy accepts updates, while a secondary keeps a read-only copy received through zone transfer.

DNSSEC uses signed DNS data so a validating resolver can detect forged or altered records. It protects integrity and origin, not query privacy. DNS over HTTPS, or DoH, uses HTTPS, typically port 443, to encrypt DNS traffic. DNS over TLS, or DoT, uses TLS, typically port 853. Both hide the query in transit, but they do not prove the answer is genuine without DNSSEC validation.

Use an A record for a name's IPv4 address and AAAA for IPv6. CNAME makes one name an alias for another name. MX points email delivery to a mail server, TXT stores text such as SPF policy or verification data, NS identifies zone name servers, and PTR maps an address back to a name.

A forward zone maps names to addresses. A reverse zone maps addresses to names through PTR records. A local hosts file can answer a name before the system asks DNS, so an old or malicious entry can make DNS troubleshooting look confusing. DNS cache time-to-live is a cache lifetime. It is not the IP packet TTL or hop limit.

Keep time trustworthy

NTP synchronizes ordinary network clocks, normally over UDP 123. It helps logs line up and keeps certificate validity checks meaningful. Precision Time Protocol, or PTP, targets much tighter timing and often needs hardware support in specialized environments. Network Time Security, or NTS, adds authenticated protection for NTP exchanges.

Analogy

Picture a new employee arriving at an office. DHCP gives the employee a desk number, directions out of the building, and a directory number. SLAAC gives the building's room pattern and lets the employee form a room label. DNS is the directory, and NTP is the building clock. Real protocols use structured messages and security checks, so the comparison ends there.

A story: Maya joins the training Wi-Fi

  1. Maya's laptop joins the training VLAN and sends a DHCP Discover. The relay forwards it to the central server.
  2. The server offers a free address from the training scope. After Maya requests it, the acknowledgement supplies the address, gateway, DNS resolver, and lease time.
  3. Maya enters study.example. Her resolver may return a cached, non-authoritative answer or query the DNS hierarchy until it receives an authoritative A or AAAA record.
  4. Her laptop synchronizes with NTP before an HTTPS sign-in. A badly wrong clock can make a valid certificate appear expired or not yet valid.

Choose the right VPN and remote management method: site-to-site vs client, split vs full tunnel, in-band vs out-of-band.

The job: Remote access should let the right person reach the right resource through a protected path, while leaving administrators a way to recover devices when normal networking fails.

Connect people and sites

A site-to-site VPN links whole networks through gateways such as routers or firewalls. It is useful when two offices regularly need to reach each other, and users often do not know a tunnel exists. A client-to-site VPN connects one user's device to an organization's network. It usually needs a VPN client, authentication, and a policy defining which internal resources that user may reach.

A clientless VPN gives a remote user limited access through a browser over TLS. It can work well for a web application or portal, but it cannot replace every application that needs a full network connection or a specialized client.

With a full-tunnel VPN, the remote device sends all traffic, including internet-bound traffic, through the organization. Security tools can inspect and apply policy to that traffic, though the organization carries the bandwidth. With a split tunnel, traffic for internal resources enters the VPN and ordinary internet traffic exits locally. Split tunneling reduces VPN load but gives the organization less visibility and control over the device's other traffic.

Pick a management connection

SSH provides an encrypted command-line connection and is the normal replacement for insecure remote terminal protocols. A GUI gives administrators a graphical management interface, often through HTTPS. An API lets tools exchange commands or configuration data, which supports automation. A console connection uses a local serial or USB path. It is valuable for first setup and for recovery when IP connectivity does not work.

The correct connection depends on the task. An API is built for repeatable tool-to-tool work. SSH suits an interactive command line. A console connection can reach a device with no working network configuration. A web GUI may be convenient but needs the same access controls and patching attention as other management interfaces.

Protect the management path

A jump box, or jump host, is a hardened management system that administrators connect to first. From there, they reach infrastructure on a restricted management network. It reduces the number of places that can directly administer devices and creates a useful audit point. Access should still require strong authentication and appropriate authorization.

In-band management uses the same production network as normal traffic. It is convenient, but an outage or bad routing change may cut off the administrator too. Out-of-band management uses a separate path such as a console server, dedicated management link, or cellular connection. It costs more to maintain but can reach equipment during a production outage.

Analogy

A VPN is like a controlled entrance between buildings. A site-to-site entrance connects two buildings, while a client entrance admits one visitor. A jump host is the staffed security desk before the equipment room. The comparison stops at encryption and routing, which have no physical-door equivalent.

A story: repairing a branch router

  1. Leah deploys a bad route to a branch router and loses the normal in-band SSH path.
  2. She uses the organization’s out-of-band console service to reach the router without depending on the failed production path.
  3. From the console, she restores the known-good configuration and verifies that in-band SSH is available again.
  4. Later, the team connects to the branch through its site-to-site VPN. Remote employees use client-to-site VPNs, and their policy sends corporate traffic through the tunnel while local internet traffic stays local.
