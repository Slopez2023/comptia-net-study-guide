Define the CIA triad and risk terms, compare AAA and authentication protocols, and know why we segment IoT, OT, guest and BYOD devices.

The job: Security is about making useful network access hard for the wrong person and manageable for the right one. You will connect protection choices to the business risk they reduce.

Protect data and prove who is at the other end

Encryption in transit protects traffic while it crosses a network: TLS, SSH, and IPsec are common examples. Encryption at rest protects a disk, database, backup, or lost laptop; use both because a secure connection does not protect a stolen drive.

PKI gives certificates a chain of trust. A certificate authority signs a public certificate so clients can verify the server identity; a self-signed certificate has no trusted outside signer, so clients normally warn about it.

Authenticate first, then limit the access

Authentication answers “Who are you?” and authorization answers “What may you do?” MFA needs factors from different categories, such as a password plus a phone prompt; two passwords are still one factor. SSO lets a user authenticate once and use several applications, often by passing a SAML assertion between a trusted identity provider and web application. Time-based authentication can mean a rotating code or a policy that permits login only at approved times.

Directories store and look up identity information. LDAP is the directory-query protocol, commonly secured as LDAPS. RADIUS is the usual AAA choice for user network access such as 802.1X Wi-Fi or VPN. TACACS+ is commonly used for administrators logging in to routers and switches because it separates AAA functions and can authorize individual commands.

Least privilege grants only the access needed for the task. RBAC makes that scalable: assign permissions to a job role, then assign people to the role instead of building each person’s permissions one by one.

Put controls where they help

Geofencing is a logical location rule: for example, deny an account login outside countries where staff work. Physical controls still matter: cameras provide evidence and deterrence, while locks limit entry to rooms, racks, and equipment. Neither replaces the other.

A honeypot is a tempting decoy system; a honeynet is a group of decoys that can reveal attacker methods. They are for detection and study, not a substitute for protecting real systems.

Keep the risk chain straight: a vulnerability is a weakness, a threat is a possible cause of harm, and an exploit uses the weakness. Risk weighs how likely that chain is against its impact. The CIA triad asks whether a choice preserves confidentiality, integrity, and availability.

Meet obligations and contain device risk

Audits check whether controls meet a rule, policy, or contract. Data locality requires data to remain in a particular geographic jurisdiction. PCI DSS governs systems that handle payment-card data, and GDPR sets obligations around personal data connected to people in the EU.

Segmentation limits a compromise to a smaller area. Put IoT and industrial IoT, plus SCADA, ICS, and other OT equipment, in tightly controlled zones because they can be difficult to patch and may operate physical processes. Separate guest and BYOD devices too: they are not automatically as trusted as managed corporate endpoints.

Analogy

Think of a hospital: an ID badge gets you through the front door, but your role opens only the rooms you need; cameras and locked drug cabinets add other layers. A network has no single physical hallway, so its rules must be enforced at many connections and services.

Recognize each attack from its symptom, and name the defense that stops it.

The job: Attack questions give you a symptom, not a vocabulary word. Learn the pattern, then choose the defense that interrupts that specific attack.

Overwhelm, escape, or confuse the network

A DoS attack overwhelms a service or link so legitimate users cannot use it. A DDoS does the same from many distributed systems, often a botnet, so filtering one source is not enough; rate limits, a CDN, or a scrubbing provider can help.

The impact is primarily an availability failure: a site may be reachable but too slow to serve its real users. Start by identifying whether the pressure is on the link, firewall, server, or application, then use controls appropriate to that location. Do not confuse a busy service with a DDoS just because the symptom is slowness; monitoring and baseline traffic data provide the evidence.

For the exam, choose the answer that protects the affected resource rather than a control that merely records the event. Logging is useful after detection, but a rate limit or upstream mitigation is the direct availability defense.

VLAN hopping tries to cross VLAN boundaries by negotiating an unauthorized trunk or abusing double-tagged frames. Disable automatic trunk negotiation, explicitly make user ports access ports, and use an unused native VLAN. MAC flooding fills a switch CAM table so the switch floods traffic; port security limits acceptable MAC addresses on a port.

ARP poisoning or ARP spoofing sends false IP-to-MAC mappings, often making the attacker appear to be the default gateway. Dynamic ARP inspection checks ARP messages against trusted address bindings and helps stop that on-path position.

Redirect the victim

DNS poisoning or DNS spoofing provides a false name-to-address answer, steering a user to a malicious destination. DNSSEC helps resolvers detect altered signed records; a secure, well-managed resolver also matters.

An evil twin is a fake wireless access point using a familiar network name to trick users into connecting. An on-path attack places an attacker between endpoints to observe or alter traffic; an evil twin can enable one, but they are not the same term. Strong enterprise Wi-Fi authentication, wireless detection, and end-to-end TLS reduce exposure.

Malware is hostile software. A virus needs a host action, a worm can spread itself, a trojan pretends to be legitimate, and ransomware denies access to data. Patching, endpoint protection, tested backups, and segmentation reduce both entry and spread.

Watch for unauthorized services

A rogue DHCP server can hand out the wrong gateway or DNS server, silently redirecting clients. DHCP snooping permits server replies only from trusted ports. A rogue AP is any unauthorized access point on the network; NAC/802.1X, port controls, and wireless monitoring help find or prevent it.

Social engineering attacks target people: phishing uses a deceptive message, dumpster diving searches discarded material, shoulder surfing watches a screen or keyboard, and tailgating follows an authorized person through a controlled entrance.

Analogy

A poisoned address book is dangerous because it sends you to the wrong house even when you read it correctly; that is DNS poisoning. It stops matching when you remember that DNS answers are exchanged automatically by systems, not copied into a paper book.

  3. He enables DHCP snooping so only the real server-facing port is trusted.

Harden devices, control who can plug in (NAC), write ACLs in the right order, and place servers in the right zone.

The job: Defenses are strongest when they prevent unnecessary access before an attacker can use it. In scenario questions, choose the feature that enforces the requested boundary.

Start with a smaller attack surface

Hardening removes avoidable opportunities: disable unused switch ports and services, change default passwords, and apply supported updates. An unused port should be shut down and placed in an unused VLAN; leaving it active is an invitation for an unknown device.

Review a device after installation instead of assuming its defaults are safe. Remove or disable management methods that are not needed, prefer protected management paths, and give administrators individual accounts so actions can be traced. Hardening is a continuing baseline: a secure configuration today needs review after firmware changes, staff changes, and new services.

Keep a documented baseline so an unexpected setting can be found and corrected rather than accepted as normal.

NAC decides whether and how a device may join. 802.1X uses a client supplicant, a switch or AP authenticator, and a RADIUS server before network access is granted. Port security limits MAC addresses on a port, while MAC filtering compares a MAC to an allow list; MAC addresses can be spoofed, so filtering alone is weak.

Protect the cryptographic keys and write precise rules

Key management covers the full key life cycle: generate keys securely, store them safely, distribute them only to authorized systems, rotate them, and revoke or destroy them when necessary. Strong encryption is not useful if an attacker can copy its key.

An ACL is an ordered allow/deny rule list. Rules are tested from top to bottom, the first matching rule wins, and an implicit deny catches traffic that no rule allowed. Put a narrow, necessary allow before a broad deny that would otherwise catch it.

Read an ACL as a packet would: check the source, destination, protocol, and port against each rule until one matches. A broad allow placed too early can quietly expose too much, just as a broad deny placed too early can block a needed application. Document the business reason for each exception so obsolete access can be removed later.

URL filtering blocks destinations by URL, domain, or category. Content filtering examines the material itself, such as blocking malware, file types, or unacceptable content. A web category block is not the same as inspecting a downloaded file.

Use zones to control trust

A trusted zone contains systems under stronger organizational control; an untrusted zone includes the internet and unknown networks. A screened subnet, often called a DMZ, is a separate zone for public-facing services such as web, mail, or public DNS. Firewall policy allows the required internet-to-DMZ traffic without exposing the internal network directly.

Analogy

A screened subnet is a building lobby: visitors can reach the reception desk without walking into staff offices. The analogy stops matching because a firewall can apply detailed rules to each kind of network conversation, not just decide who enters the building.

A story: Priya publishes a customer portal

  1. Priya places the public web server in a screened subnet, not on the internal LAN.
  2. She allows HTTPS from the internet to that server, then permits only the exact backend connection the server needs.
  3. She places the specific allow above broader deny rules in the firewall ACL.
  4. She disables unused services and rotates the server’s keys as part of its hardening plan.
Keep these separate
  • 802.1X: authenticates access to a port or wireless network.
  • Port security: restricts MAC addresses at a switch port.
  • URL filtering: restricts where web traffic goes.
  • Content filtering: restricts what the traffic contains.
  • Hardening: disable unused ports (shut them down and put them in an unused VLAN), disable unused services (Telnet, HTTP), change default passwords, patch firmware.
  • NAC: 802.1X = port-based authentication (supplicant → authenticator switch/AP → RADIUS server). Port security = limit or learn MACs per port. MAC filtering = an allow list of MACs (easy to spoof, weak on its own).
  • ACLs are read top-down, and the first match wins. Put specific rules first. Every ACL ends with an implicit deny all.
  • URL filtering = block sites by address or category. Content filtering = block by what the content contains (malware, file types).
  • Key management = generate, store, rotate and revoke encryption keys safely.
Watch for

A PBQ shows a firewall rule list where traffic is wrongly blocked. Look for a broad deny above the specific allow, and move the allow up.
